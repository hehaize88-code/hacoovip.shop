ACBuy Image Search: Find a Product from a Photo

Use ACBuy image search to trace an item from a screenshot, compare similar listings and check the exact variant before placing an order.

Updated 2 November 2026

A photo of a backpack on a train seat might tell you exactly what you like about it without giving you a single useful search term. ACBuy lists image search among the ways to find similar products in its official shopping guide. Start with a clear view of one object. The useful part comes after the search, when you work out which result shows the feature you wanted and whether the seller actually offers that version.

Start with the item you can describe

Before uploading anything, write a short description in your own words. For a bag it might be “rectangular backpack, two short handles, large front opening, narrow shoulder straps.” Then underline the feature that matters most to you. Someone buying a bag for its shape may accept several designs. Someone carrying a particular laptop needs evidence about the compartment as well as a visual resemblance. Those two shoppers can use the same photograph and reasonably pick different results.

Look for the least obstructed view in the original post. If a video has several frames, choose the one where the object is still and its edges are visible. A sharp side view is often more informative than a blurry close-up of the front. Keep the original to refer back to. Make a separate copy for searching so that you can try another crop later without losing the handle, hem or closure you cut off the first time.

Make two crops that answer different questions

For the first crop, include the whole object and a small margin. An outfit photo may contain a jacket, trousers, a belt and the bag in your hand. Crop away everything you reasonably can while keeping both ends of the bag and the points where the straps attach. If you leave only the buckle, results may quite sensibly show spare buckles. If you leave the whole outfit, you may spend time comparing jackets.

For the second crop, keep the part that distinguishes one design from another. On a backpack, that could be the back panel and the way the pockets open. On a jacket, try the collar together with the upper pockets. Keep this detail view for comparing results or for a second search. Remove captions, contact details, shipping labels and unrelated faces from the copy. Leave the colour and shape alone. A filter that turns navy into black adds uncertainty when you later compare colours.

Read the listings behind the thumbnails

Use the image-search option on ACBuy if it is available in the interface you are using. Start with the whole-item crop. Open two or three promising product pages and compare the same view across all of them. Keep a note of the seller, listing title, chosen option and the feature you could actually see. Three different sellers can display the same promotional photograph, so three matching thumbnails may give you only one piece of evidence.

Check the second and third photographs, the options and the description. If the front and side views appear to show different pocket arrangements, see whether the seller has grouped several models under one listing. Select a particular option and check what changes. Sometimes the title covers an entire range while the photograph beside one option shows a smaller size or an accessory. Save the option wording along with the link. A bookmark alone will not tell you which one you compared.

A real comparison using two Kånken backpacks

Fjällräven’s classic Kånken and its Kånken Laptop 15 illustrate the limit of a front photograph. Both have a small zipped front pocket and side pockets. The manufacturer describes slender straps on the classic and padded straps and a separate padded laptop compartment on the Laptop 15. A front view that shows the front pocket and little else could plausibly lead you to either model.

Suppose your reference is a blue bag hanging by its handles and you want the version with the separate laptop compartment. A listing that reproduces the blue front view earns a place on your shortlist. Now look for the back and an opened view of the compartment. A photo of a foam seat pad is inconclusive because both models include one. If the seller only shows the front, record the laptop compartment as unconfirmed. The result may still suit someone who wants that shape for everyday use. It gives you too little information for your specific purchase.

Apply the same comparison to another category without relying on logos. On a zipped jacket, count the front pockets, follow the lower edge of the zip and see how the cuffs close. Compare a left-side view with another left-side view, accounting for mirrored selfies. A dark shape that looks like a pocket in one image may turn out to be a fold in the fabric once you see a flat view.

Separate the pictured item from the option for sale

After you have a plausible match, read the selected option as if the first photo were hidden. What exactly would the seller send? Check the colour name, dimensions, quantity, included pieces and price for that selection. For a bag pictured with a detachable strap, check whether the strap is included, optional or the only item covered by the lowest displayed price. A staged photograph alone cannot settle any of those details.

If an important specification is missing, ask a narrow question that identifies the option and the missing feature. For example, “For the blue version shown in option B, can you show the separate compartment open next to the back panel?” This gives the seller or agent something concrete to check. A general request for more photos can produce another front view that leaves your original question unanswered. Keep the reply with the listing so you can recognise any later change.

If the search keeps finding the wrong thing

Notice the pattern before changing the picture. A page of jacket results when you wanted a bag suggests the jacket occupies too much of your input. A page of straps suggests the crop is too tight. Several near-identical bags with the wrong closure suggest the original angle hides the relevant detail. Change one thing on the next attempt so that you can see which change helped.

If a second angle brings the same ambiguity, change how you search. ACBuy’s guide also describes searching by product name and submitting an existing marketplace link. Try a short description built from the details you observed, such as “rectangular backpack padded straps laptop compartment.” Add a model name only when a reliable label or specification supports it. If you already have the seller’s product page, start there and compare its variant photos directly.

Keep useful evidence for the warehouse check

Save the source picture, selected-option photo, listing address and a one-sentence note on the feature that mattered. If you order and the item later reaches the warehouse, compare its inspection photos with that record. Begin with details the camera can show, such as the fastening and pocket layout. If the crucial view is missing, ask about an additional photo before making a parcel decision. A warehouse image may confirm the visible construction. It cannot establish the fabric composition or authenticity on its own.

End your search when you can explain your choice in one sentence. “The back view shows the padded straps, the open photo shows the separate sleeve and both belong to the blue option I selected” is a useful buying note. “It looked like the first result” leaves every important detail for the parcel to reveal.
