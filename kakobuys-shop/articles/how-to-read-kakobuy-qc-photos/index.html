QC field guide

How to Read Kakobuy QC Photos Before You Ship

A practical inspection order for shape, measurements, stitching, labels and seller-link risk—without pretending a photo can prove everything.

By Kakobuys.shop Research Desk11 min readReviewed July 29, 2026

A warehouse QC photo is most useful when it answers a specific question. “Does this look good?” is too broad. “Do both shoes have the same toe shape?” or “Does the measured chest width match the ordered size?” can be answered with visible evidence. That distinction prevents a common mistake: treating a clean-looking photograph as proof that every important detail is correct.

The short versionReview in this order: ordered variant, overall shape, measurements, construction, labels and visible damage. Stop when a missing view makes the next check impossible.

1. Confirm that the photos belong to your order

Before judging quality, compare the colour, size, model and quantity shown in the warehouse record with the option you selected. A perfectly photographed wrong variant is still a wrong order. Look for the size tag, packaging label and any visible item code. If the listing has been edited since purchase, preserve a copy of the option shown in your order details rather than relying on the seller’s current page.

Photo mix-ups are not theoretical. A Kakobuy QC image notice published on May 10, 2026 acknowledged that some product QC images had displayed abnormally, shown incorrect images or been temporarily unavailable. That dated notice is a useful reason to check identity before examining small details.

2. Begin with the whole silhouette

Start with the widest, straightest image. For footwear, compare the left and right toe shape, heel height and sole line. For clothing, check whether the body, sleeves and shoulders sit symmetrically when laid flat. For bags, compare the top line, side panels and handle placement. An obvious proportion problem is more important than a tiny loose thread because it affects the entire appearance and is difficult to correct.

Perspective matters. A phone held slightly off-centre can make one side appear longer. Use table edges, measurement mats or other straight background lines to judge whether the camera is square to the item. If the angle is distorted, request a straighter view before deciding that the item itself is asymmetric.

3. Treat measurements as evidence, not size labels

A label that says “L” is not a measurement. Compare the seller’s size chart with an actual warehouse measurement of the garment laid flat. Chest width, total length, sleeve length, waist and inseam are usually more useful than the printed size. Allow for the measurement method: pulling the fabric, measuring along a curve or starting at the wrong seam can change the result.

Compare those numbers with a similar item you already own, measured in the same way. This is more dependable than converting between regional size names. If a measurement is close to your minimum acceptable size, remember that camera photos do not tell you how much a fabric stretches or how it changes after washing.

4. Move from construction to surface details

Once the silhouette and size are acceptable, inspect seams, panel alignment, edge paint, zip tracks, print placement and hardware. Follow long lines: a crooked seam is easier to detect when you trace it from one edge to the other. Compare repeated elements such as eyelets, pockets or printed motifs. Inconsistent spacing often reveals a construction problem that a front-facing photo hides.

Lighting can create false alarms. Reflections make metal look scratched, direct flash makes dark fabric appear grey and strong sharpening can exaggerate texture. Ask for a close-up in neutral light when a possible defect appears in only one image. Do not confuse a limitation of the photograph with a confirmed flaw.

5. Separate “visible” from “not proven”

A photo can show that a zipper is visibly installed; it cannot prove the zipper will survive months of use. It can show a fabric surface; it cannot confirm fibre percentages without trustworthy documentation. It can show a logo or label; it cannot establish authenticity. It cannot reliably prove waterproofing, battery safety, long-term colourfastness or exact comfort.

This boundary is important because many spreadsheet pages use phrases such as “verified quality” without explaining what was verified. A more accurate statement is “the visible areas in these photos were reviewed for the listed defects.” That is narrower, but it is useful.

6. Decide while after-sales options remain open

Kakobuy’s publicly indexed help entry describes a five-day return/exchange guarantee for eligible items offered by participating third-party sellers. That does not mean every order can be returned. Marketplace restrictions, customised goods, seller participation and the condition of the item can affect eligibility. Check the order’s actual after-sales state and current deadline before assuming a return is available.

If you report a problem, describe one observable issue per sentence and point to the relevant photo. “The measured chest width is 52 cm; the ordered size chart lists 57 cm” is stronger than “the size looks bad.” Save the order option, measurement image and response in the same record.

A repeatable ten-point QC checklist

  1. Match the order number, item type, colour and quantity.
  2. Confirm the ordered size or specification label.
  3. Review the full front, rear and side silhouette.
  4. Compare repeated parts for symmetry.
  5. Check measurements against the seller’s chart.
  6. Trace long seams, edges, soles or zip lines.
  7. Inspect prints, embroidery, hardware and labels.
  8. Look for stains, dents, tears or transit damage.
  9. Request a neutral close-up when evidence is unclear.
  10. Resolve the issue before parcel submission when possible.

When an extra photo is worth requesting

Ask for another photo when it can change the decision. A measurement photo is useful if the listed size is close to your limit. A straight rear view is useful when one heel appears tilted in an angled image. A close-up is useful when a possible scratch, stain or broken stitch appears in only one frame. “More photos” without a defined target can produce more images without better evidence.

Phrase the request so the photographer knows the angle and reference: “Please photograph both heel tabs together, camera centred at the same height,” or “Please show the tape from the left underarm seam to the right underarm seam without stretching the fabric.” Keep the request short. A specific instruction reduces the chance that the new photo repeats the original limitation.

Red flags in a spreadsheet entry

Be cautious when the product title is vague, the destination changes to an unrelated item, all QC examples come from different colours or sellers, or a quality claim has no review date. A link that once produced a good item can change ownership, variants or pricing. A large number of community saves does not prove that the current destination is unchanged.

Price is also context, not a verdict. A higher price does not guarantee better construction, while an unusually low price can reflect a clearance option, missing accessories or a different specification. Compare the selected variant shown at checkout, not just the lowest price printed on a search card.

Keep a small evidence record

Save the seller URL, marketplace item ID, ordered option, size chart and the warehouse images used for the decision. If a problem appears later, this record makes it possible to explain what changed. It also helps you evaluate a seller across several documented orders instead of relying on memory or a community label.

A useful record does not need a complicated spreadsheet. One folder or note per order is enough: purchase date, destination URL, chosen variant, arrival date, visible issues, after-sales deadline and final decision. This habit is more reliable than assuming the public spreadsheet will preserve the same row forever.

The goal is not to turn a few photographs into certainty. It is to make a better decision from the evidence actually available, document what remains uncertain and act before the relevant deadline closes.
