Compare bracelet sizes

Oopbuy bracelets by wrist size and clasp type

An 18 cm bracelet and a bracelet for an 18 cm wrist can be two different purchases. Before choosing an Oopbuy option, find out whose measurement the number describes. Then look at how the bracelet goes on. A chain that opens at the wrist can sit fairly close once fastened. A closed bangle has to clear your hand first. Three listings from Oopbuy’s bracelet catalogue show how to turn those distinctions into questions a seller can actually answer.

Start with the wrist that will wear it

Turn your palm up and rest your arm. Wrap a narrow, flexible tape just above the wrist bone, where you want the bracelet to sit. Keep it in contact with your skin all the way around, with no finger underneath and no pressure marks. Read the number where the tape crosses its starting point. If you only have a ruler, wrap a strip of plain paper around the same spot, mark the overlap and measure from the end to your mark.

Write down the wrist, the position and the units. For example, “left wrist, above the bone, 16.4 cm” gives you a measurement you can reproduce. If the seller supplies a chart, check its measuring instructions. Pandora illustrates measuring above the bone, while Cartier’s printable sizer uses the thickest part of the wrist. For a model with its own instructions, remeasure at the indicated point. If you use a printable ruler, check its scale against the reference on the page.

Let a favourite bracelet tell you how much movement you like

Choose something you wear for a full day. Note whether it stays beside your watch, slides towards your hand when you reach down, or turns until the decorative face sits underneath. Which of those habits would you keep? A fine chain can slide over the wrist bone in a way you enjoy, while a wide bracelet doing the same thing may sit awkwardly against a keyboard.

For a flexible chain, open the clasp and lay it beside a ruler. Measure from the part of the clasp that meets the fastening ring to the ring you actually use. Record the full end-to-end length separately if the new seller quotes it that way. Photograph the ruler and both ends together. For a rigid piece, record the inside width and depth instead. An outside measurement includes the walls of the bracelet, so it tells you little about the available space.

Use wrist size to compare lengths

Suppose your wrist measures 16 cm and the thin chain you like measures 17.5 cm to its usual fastening point. The difference is 1.5 cm. You now have a sensible target when comparing similar chains. A bracelet with thick links or large beads takes up some of that space on the inside. For that design, ask the seller for the closed inner circumference or a wrist range tested on the same version.

The examples use a hypothetical 1.5 cm difference throughout so you can see the arithmetic. Substitute the difference you measured on your own bracelet. In each row, the candidate length is measured to the fastening point you would use.

Match the clasp to your hands and routine

A lobster clasp has a lever that opens a small jaw. On a chain with an extender, your choice of ring determines both the wearing length and the amount of chain left dangling. Ask for a close-up of the last usable ring. If you prefer a tidy wrist beside a watch, look at what hangs below that ring, including any tag. A round spring-ring clasp also has a small lever. Look at the size of the tab if you usually fasten jewellery with your other hand.

With a toggle, the bar has to turn and pass through its ring. A chain that sits beautifully once closed can leave too little slack for that last movement. If you already own a toggle, try fastening it at the spot you measured and note the room your fingers need. With a box clasp, look for the tongue that slides into the box and check whether a side catch needs a second movement. A photograph of the mechanism open is more informative than a front view of the centrepiece.

Check the opening on sliders, cuffs and bangles

For a sliding cord or bead adjustment, ask for two measurements. How small is the closed loop at its shortest wearable setting, and how large can it open to pass over your hand? Bring your thumb towards your little finger and measure around the widest part of the folded hand. Compare that measurement with the opening of a cord bracelet you can put on easily. Also check how far the cord ends hang at your preferred setting.

A closed rigid bangle is easier to compare with another bangle. Place one that passes over your hand on paper and trace its inner edge. For an oval, measure the opening across its widest point and again at right angles. A hinged bangle opens at the side, so also ask how far it opens and where the hinge sits. For an open cuff, measure the gap between the tips as well as its internal dimensions. Ask whether the maker allows reshaping before counting on a narrower gap.

Read three Oopbuy listings with different questions in mind

The red knotted cord bracelet, item 743383774558, appears in Oopbuy’s catalogue under a red rope and diamond-knot description. “Diamond knot” describes the knot pattern in that title. The title alone leaves the fastening method unclear. Ask to see the underside and both cord ends. If they slide, request minimum and maximum loop measurements. If the ends tie, ask for the cord length left on either side when tied on a wrist your size.

The modular heart design, item 949751438498, is listed with a double hook and pendant chain. A modular jewellery listing can offer a component as one of its options. Before comparing sizes, ask exactly what the selected option includes. For a complete band, request its assembled, resting inside measurement and ask whether links can be removed. For a component, measure the link on the bracelet you already own and compare the attachment points. A matching heart shape tells you nothing about that connection.

The tennis-style chain, item 1057429422314, has “3–6 mm” in its catalogue title. That number gives you no wrist length. Ask which dimension it describes for your option, then ask for the wearable length and a side view of the fastening. If the seller offers several widths, compare a strip of paper cut to each width on your wrist. That quick test shows how much wrist the design covers before you decide which length and clasp details to pursue.

Get a measurement you can use before ordering

A useful request names one item, one variant and one measurement. For example, “For the 17 cm option, could you show the opened bracelet beside a ruler with the clasp and fastening ring visible? My wrist measures 16 cm. I wear a similar chain at 17.5 cm.” If it is a bangle, ask for a photo of a ruler across the inside of the opening. Make clear which axis you want measured when the outline is oval.

When you read the answer, check the ruler’s zero mark, the far end and the unit. Seventeen centimetres and 170 millimetres are the same length. An image that crops out the clasp can still leave you guessing about a full centimetre of hardware. Keep the answer with the exact option name. If you change the width, finish or charm combination at checkout, make sure the size information still refers to that option.

Buying a gift or checking the delivered bracelet

For a gift, borrowing a bracelet of the same construction gives you a discreet reference. Measure the fastening ring the wearer uses or trace the opening of their everyday bangle. If all you know is a wrist size, an adjustable design is easier to assess when the seller provides both ends of the range. On the smallest setting, check the leftover tail. On the largest, check that there is a usable ring or a secure stopping point.

When the bracelet arrives, measure it by the same method you used to choose it. Try fastening it yourself, resting your wrist on a table and slipping on a sleeve. If the result surprises you, compare the measurements before changing links or bending a cuff. The point of your notes is simple. They let you tell whether you chose a length you dislike or received a different size from the one you selected.
