Sugargoo Hoodies Spreadsheet UK: Measurements and QC Guide

Compare garments by consistent measurements and visible evidence, with clear distinctions between hoodies, crew necks and knitwear.

By Sugargoo VIP Editorial Team · Published 4 October 2026 · Service guidance checked 4 October 2026

The most useful detail in a Sugargoo hoodies spreadsheet is often a measurement, not a size letter. Two tops labelled medium can have different chest widths, body lengths, sleeves and intended fits. For UK buyers ordering without trying an item on, a clear comparison with a garment you already own is more informative than a generic “true to size” label.

Our hoodies and sweaters category currently groups several kinds of tops, including crew necks and knitwear. It is not a list of five hooded sweatshirts. This guide shows how to use those existing records honestly, build a measurement sheet and request the evidence needed before international shipping.

Identify the garment before comparing it

Begin with construction: hooded or crew neck, pullover or zipped, knitted or sweatshirt-style, short or long body, standard or dropped shoulder. A photograph can suggest these features, but the live listing should explain the actual option being sold. Save the selected option text when one page combines different cuts or colourways.

Do not transfer material claims from a category label to every item in it. “Cotton,” “heavyweight,” “fleece” and a stated fabric weight are claims to verify for the selected garment. A thick-looking photograph does not establish grams per square metre, fibre composition, warmth or shrinkage after washing.

The same caution applies to brand names in seller titles. They identify how the listing describes itself; they do not make this site an authenticator or a physical reviewer. Keep seller descriptions in one field and independently observed evidence in another.

Open the five existing category records

These links lead to the local product records, which then connect to the current main-site listings. The notes explain what each stored title leaves to verify. They do not report a test purchase, a measured specification or present stock.

If you specifically want a hood, filter candidates by that requirement first. Keeping a mixed category visible is useful for discovery, but it should not blur the difference between a sweater and a hoodie when placing an order.

Measure a reference top before choosing a size

Lay a garment you like on a flat surface without deliberately stretching it. Record chest width, body length and sleeve length with a note describing the endpoints. Take a simple reference photograph for your own use so you can repeat the method later. Compare like with like: a fitted knit and an oversized sweatshirt may serve different purposes.

Match those definitions to the seller's chart. If the chart does not explain a dimension, ask before ordering. Do not silently interpret a number as inches when the listing uses centimetres, or turn a flat garment width into a fit promise. The value of the sheet comes from consistent methods.

Set a fit range that reflects your own preference

Write the intended use beside the reference measurements: close fit, room for a T-shirt, or space for another layer. Then decide which dimensions are essential. Someone who dislikes a short body may prioritise length over a small chest-width difference; another buyer may care most about sleeve reach.

For a hypothetical example, a reference sweatshirt measures 58 cm across the chest and 68 cm in body length using your chosen method. A listing chart showing 61 cm and 64 cm suggests a wider, shorter shape. It does not prove that the garment is defective or that everyone should change size. It shows why one size letter cannot express both dimensions.

This is a comparison example, not a specification for any linked find. Set your own acceptable range and keep the seller's stated measurement tolerance separate. If the size chart leaves a critical dimension unresolved, mark the row incomplete rather than inventing a value from an image.

Ask for warehouse measurements with visible endpoints

Once the correct item has arrived, locate its images using the warehouse QC photo finder guide. Match the size label and colour to your order, then decide whether the standard evidence resolves your fit questions. A labelled medium can still have measurements you do not want.

Request the specific measurement that changes your decision. For chest width, explain that the garment should lie flat without stretching and that both tape endpoints should be visible. For body length, name the starting point. For a dropped shoulder, specify how sleeve length should be measured rather than assuming staff will use your reference method.

Read the available service description and price before confirming a request. Do not assume that every standard QC photo includes measurements. When a result looks inconsistent, first check positioning, units and endpoints; an unclear method should be clarified before you treat the number as decisive.

Inspect visible details without overstating fabric quality

Use overall front and back views to check identity, colour distribution, major marks and the position of visible design details. Look more closely at seams, cuffs, collar or hood attachment, pocket openings and any fastener. If a print or embroidery placement is important, compare it with the selected listing option, not a different version found elsewhere.

Lighting can change how dark colours and textures appear. Ask for clarification when a shadow could be mistaken for a stain. A clear close-up may show a loose thread or visible hole, but photographs cannot establish wash performance, long-term pilling, colourfastness or warmth in British weather.

For composition, keep the seller description, care-label image and any reliable documentation distinct. Even a readable label is a statement attached to the product, not a laboratory analysis. Our QC inspection guide explains how to record a visible concern and request evidence proportionate to the issue.

Plan packing around the actual garment

A bulky sweatshirt and a delicate decorated knit may need different handling. Ask about suitable packing rather than selecting compression for every item in the category. Consider whether folding, pressure or contact with shoe hardware could damage visible details. Separate garments when the proposed mixed parcel creates an avoidable risk.

Record item weight only when it has been measured, and distinguish it from final parcel weight. Protective materials, retained packaging and the parcel dimensions can change the chargeable amount. Sugargoo's published pre-shipment simulation guidance describes checking actual and volumetric weight before shipment; it does not make every clothing parcel cheaper automatically.

Compare the same contents and packing preferences when evaluating UK routes. The shipping calculator guide helps separate estimated inputs from final packed figures. If a garment is waiting on a return decision, leave it out of a supposedly final parcel estimate.

Finish with an item-level decision

Your completed row should show the exact variant, chart used, reference measurements, warehouse measurements where requested, visible issues and the action taken. “Ready for packing” should mean the information you considered essential is resolved. “Needs length measurement” is a more useful status than an unexplained tick.

Check the applicable after-sales deadline before waiting for another item to arrive. If the garment does not meet your requirements, use the return evidence guide to organise the issue while the available options can still be checked. Keep the original row after a return or replacement so the new item is not accidentally approved using the old photographs.
